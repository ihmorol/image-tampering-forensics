## fontani2013

**Full citation:** M. Fontani, T. Bianchi, A. De Rosa, A. Piva, M. Barni, "A Framework for Decision Fusion in Image Forensics based on Dempster-Shafer Theory of Evidence," draft dated Feb 27 2013, Copyright (c) 2013 IEEE (journal name given only as "JOURNAL OF LATEX CLASS FILES" template header on p.1 — actual venue/journal name and DOI not present in the extracted PDF text; `not verified from retrieved text` for venue/DOI).
**Local file:** `references/papers/fontani2013_dempster-shafer.pdf` (32 pages), text extraction: clean

**What the method computes:**
- Frames each tool's evidence about a binary trace θ (present `t` / not-present `n`) as a Basic Belief Assignment (BBA) over the power set of {t,n}: m(t), m(n), m(t∪n=doubt) (Sec. III-B, Eq. 6).
- Each tool's raw scalar output α∈[0,1] is mapped to (μT, μN, μTN) via tool-specific trapezoidal "detection mapping" functions, fit qualitatively from histograms of the tool's output on tampered vs. original training images (Sec. III-B1, Fig. 1, Fig. 5). Where tampered/original score distributions overlap, mass goes to doubt.
- **Reliability/discounting (directly answers what we need on unavailable cues):** each tool has scalar reliability R∈[0,1]. Discounting (Eq. 7, Sec. III-B2, derived formally in Appendix, Eq. 10-12): m_Atot(t) = AR·μT, m_Atot(n) = AR·μN, m_Atot(doubt) = CA = 1 − AR(μT+μN). At AR=1 the tool's raw evidence is fully trusted; at AR=0 *all* mass collapses to doubt regardless of what the tool output — the tool is silenced, not renormalized among the others. This is the key mechanism: unreliable/inapplicable tools are down-weighted by shifting their own mass toward "don't know," not by redistributing weight to other tools.
- Tools are combined pairwise via Dempster's combination rule (Eq. 3, Sec. II-B): m12(X) = (1/(1−K))·Σ_{A∩B=X} m1(A)m2(B), where conflict K=Σ_{A∩B=∅} m1(A)m2(B) is normalized away but can be logged as a diagnostic of "how unexpected" a tool combination was.
- New tools targeting an already-modeled trace fuse directly (Eq. 8); tools targeting a new trace require extending frames via vacuous extension/marginalization (Sec. III-C2, Def. 4-5) before fusion — this is how the framework stays incrementally extensible.
- Known logical incompatibilities between traces (e.g., trace θ present implies trace φ absent) are injected as a separate compatibility BBA with one focal set = the union of allowed trace combinations (Eq. 9, Sec. III-D), fused last (order doesn't matter, Dempster's rule is commutative/associative, Eq. 4-5).
- Hierarchical fusion: traces are grouped into classes (e.g. JPEG-related, camera-related, geometry-related); belief marginalization collapses each class to tampered/non-tampered before final combination, to keep the state space from growing exponentially (Sec. III-E, Fig. 3).
- Final decision (Sec. III-F): compute Bel(T) (belief that at least one trace present) and Bel(N) (belief that no trace present) over the final BBA. Classify as tampered when Bel(T) > Bel(N) + ε; sweeping ε∈[−1,1] produces an ROC curve.
- Operates at image/region level assuming the suspect region is already known (stated explicitly Sec. III intro): "most forensic tools assume knowledge of the suspect region... forgery localization is a different problem... we will consider [it] in future works" (Sec. III, also repeated in Conclusions/Sec. V future work list). **This is a classification framework, not a per-pixel localization framework.**

**Reported performance:**
- Synthetic dataset (9600 images, 4800 tampered/4800 original, Sec. IV-A3, Table III/IV): AUC (normalized to PFA∈[0,0.3]) — DS(proposed) 0.924, SVM 0.921, OR(logical disjunction) 0.882, Handicap-SVM 0.869, OR-HR(hard-reasoning OR) 0.829 (Fig. 6a). Single-tool AUCs on same dataset: Tool A 0.288, Tool B 0.379, Tool C 0.779, Tool D 0.479, Tool E 0.514 (Fig. 6b).
- Realistic dataset (70 hand-made forgeries + 70 originals = 140 images, made by students with photo-editing software, Sec. IV-A3): AUC — DS 0.745, SVM 0.631, OR-HR 0.633, OR 0.536 (Fig. 7a, 10 train/test iterations). Single-tool AUCs: Tool A 0.256, Tool B 0.470, Tool C 0.559, Tool D 0.251, Tool E 0.538 (Fig. 7b). "except for very low probabilities of false alarms (<2%), the performance of the DST method is always better than those provided by each single tool" (Sec. IV-B).
- Reliability-perturbation robustness test (Sec. IV-B): Gaussian noise (μ=0, σ=0.15) added to reliabilities, 20 repetitions → AUC standard deviation of 0.02 on the synthetic dataset — cited by authors as evidence the framework is robust to inaccurate reliability estimates.
- Handicap-SVM (trained without Class-1 combination) accuracy on unseen Class-1 combination: drops from 77.2% (full SVM) to 18.7% (Sec. IV-B) — used by authors to argue ML-based fusion fails to generalize to unseen trace combinations, while the tool-by-tool-trained DST framework does not need cross-tool training data at all.
- Execution times (Table V, Matlab on Core2 Duo 2.26GHz/4GB RAM): framework definition (offline, done once per tool-set) grows from 0.11s (2 tools) to 39.8s (7 tools); belief evaluation (online, per-image) stays small: 0.02s (2 tools) to 1.3s (7 tools).

**Relevance to our fusion and decision layer:** This is the single most directly applicable reference for the "cue unavailable" problem: instead of renormalizing weights among remaining cues (which silently promotes whatever cue is left to full authority — exactly the failure mode named in our brief), Fontani's discounting sends the unavailable/unreliable cue's own mass to an explicit "doubt" bucket, and Dempster's rule is intrinsically forgiving of doubt-heavy BBAs (a BBA that is 100% doubt is a no-op under combination). This suggests, for our per-pixel fusion, associating each cue at each pixel with a (mass-for-tamper, mass-for-authentic, mass-for-doubt) triple driven by both its raw score and a scalar reliability, rather than a single scalar weight. The compatibility-BBA mechanism (Sec. III-D) is also relevant if we ever want to encode mutual-exclusivity between cues (e.g., a strong CFA-consistency finding vs. a strong resampling finding in the same region). Caveat: the paper solves image/region-level binary classification with a known suspect region, not dense per-pixel localization — the spatial/localization extension is explicitly left as future work, so the pixel-map adaptation is our own design work, not something to lift directly.

**Limitations the authors themselves state:**
- Operates on a known suspect region, not full-image dense localization; extending to spatial/localization fusion is listed as future work (Sec. III intro, Sec. V).
- The "realistic" test set is small (70 forgeries / 140 images total) because "creating good forgeries is a time consuming procedure" (Sec. IV-A3) — authors flag this as a dataset-size caveat.
- Independence of tools (assumption 4, Sec. III-A) is required for Dempster's rule to be valid, and the authors admit "the concept of independence in DST is not equivalent to statistical independence"; they only argue limited dependencies "would not undermine" the framework rather than proving it.
- Future-work list (Sec. V) explicitly names unresolved items: incorporating spatial information, handling counter-forensics-aware attackers, evaluating on larger realistic datasets, and comparing DST against fuzzy-theory/Bayesian/imprecise-probability alternatives — i.e., the authors do not claim DST is proven superior to all reasoning frameworks, only to the three baselines tested (OR, OR-HR, SVM).

## korus2016_random-fields

**Full citation:** P. Korus, J. Huang, "Evaluation of Random Field Models in Multi-modal Unsupervised Tampering Localization," 2016 IEEE International Workshop on Information Forensics and Security (WIFS), 2016 (IEEE ISBN/conference code 978-1-5090-1138-4/16/$31.00 given on p.1; no DOI printed in the extracted text — `not verified from retrieved text` for DOI).
**Local file:** `references/papers/korus2016_random-fields.pdf` (6 pages), text extraction: clean

**What the method computes:**
- Fuses per-pixel tampering-probability maps from a CFA detector (Ferrara et al. [7], 8×8 px non-overlapping blocks, prediction-error based, saturated blocks' scores reset) and a PRNU detector (Chen et al. [8]/Korus & Huang [13], Bayesian correlation-based, using 64×64 px analysis windows in this paper vs. the "standard" 128×128 to reduce scale mismatch with CFA) — Sec. II.
- Baseline pixel-wise fusion rules (Sec. III-A, Eq. 2-4): sum `(c_cfa+c_prnu)/2 > τ`; product `c_cfa·c_prnu / (c_cfa·c_prnu + (1-c_cfa)(1-c_prnu)) > τ`; disjunction `(c_cfa>τ) ∨ (c_prnu>τ)`; plus an empirical/learned rule (8×8 quantized-bin lookup table fit from ground-truth co-occurrence, symmetrized and smoothed). Binary outputs post-processed with morphological opening (15×15 disk SE); disjunction rule also tested with detector-specific ("independent") cleaning: 31×31 opening + 19×19 dilation on the PRNU branch.
- **CRF energy formulation (Eq. 5, Sec. III-B) — directly answers priority #2:**
  E(t) = (1/|D|) Σ_d Σ_i ψτ(c_i^(d) | t_i) + Σ_i Σ_{j∈Ξ_i} φ_p(t_i, t_j)
  where D={cfa,prnu}, ψτ is a unary potential built from each detector's calibrated score (Eq. 6-7: ψτ(c|t) = −log max(Ψmin, Ψτ(c|t)), with Ψτ a threshold-equalized piecewise-linear function of the score and a floor Ψmin=0.001 to avoid infinite energy / permanently pinned labels), and φ_p is a pairwise (Potts-like) potential penalizing label disagreement between neighboring pixels, weighted by content similarity.
  - **Grid CRF**: 8-nearest-neighbor grid, φ_p(t_i,t_j)=β_ij|t_i−t_j|, β_ij = β0 + β1·exp(−|I_i−I_j|²/2θ2²) (Eq. 8) — content-adaptive: interaction strength decays with RGB distance between pixels.
  - **Dense CRF**: fully connected (Ξ_i = all other pixels), Gaussian bilateral+spatial kernel β_ij = β0·exp(−|p_i−p_j|²/2θ0²) + β1·exp(−|p_i−p_j|²/2θ1² − |I_i−I_j|²/2θ2²) (Eq. 9a-b) — same form as DenseCRF/DenseCut image-segmentation potentials [12].
  - Labeling solved via graph cuts (grid CRF, using UGM toolbox, submodular since β0,β1≥0) or efficient mean-field inference (dense CRF, Krähenbühl & Koltun solver) — Sec. IV.
  - Empirically chosen CRF parameters given in Table I: grid (β0=0.25, β1=2.00, θ2=25), dense (β0=3.75, β1=3.00, θ0=3.5, θ1=10, θ2=20).
- Automated parameter selection procedure (Sec. IV-A): cross-validation with random 25%/75% train/test splits, grid search (grid CRF, 256 pts over [0,3]×[0,3] for β) or random parameter sampling (dense CRF, 1000 draws β~U(0,10), θ~U(0.1,50)) maximizing average F1 on the training split; M=30 splits for grid CRF, M=10 for dense CRF; threshold swept over 49 values in (0,1) (19 for training speed, cubic-interpolated).

**Reported performance:**
- Max F1 scores (Fig. 2 caption table): sum 0.57, product 0.61, disjunction(CC) 0.57, disjunction(IC) 0.60, empirical 0.61, grid CRF 0.69, dense CRF 0.68. Individual detectors: CFA 0.44, PRNU 0.49. So CRF fusion beats the best pixel-wise fusion (product/empirical, 0.61) by roughly +0.07-0.08 F1, and beats single detectors by +0.20-0.25 F1 (Sec. IV-B).
- "no major differences between the CRF models" — grid slightly better ROC, but F1 shows no difference; scatter plot of per-image peak-F1 confirms no clear winner between grid vs dense CRF (Sec. IV-B, Fig. 2 bottom-left).
- Stability/cross-validation results (Fig. 4) "nearly identical" to hand-picked-parameter results.
- Section IV-C qualitative case study (Fig. 6): for one example forgery, CRF *guided by the true tampered-region content* actually scores WORSE (F1=0.664/Acc=0.749) than a content-agnostic grid CRF (F1=0.812/Acc=0.842) or even plain pixel-wise product fusion (F1=0.844/Acc=0.866) — despite the content-guided CRF visibly tracing the object shape more precisely. This is presented explicitly as evidence that F1/accuracy can be misleading (Priority #4 relevance below).

**Relevance to our fusion and decision layer:** This is the direct source for the CRF energy formulation we'd reimplement for spatial fusion: unary term = per-pixel calibrated potential from each cue's own score (Eq. 6-7, with a floor to avoid label lock-in), pairwise term = content-adaptive smoothness (Eq. 8/9) that pulls neighboring, visually-similar pixels toward the same label, solved by graph cuts or mean-field — this replaces our single global threshold + separate morphological cleanup with a solve that is smoothness-aware and content-aware in one step, and the CRF post-processing supersedes ad hoc morphological opening (Sec. III-B: "CRF-based fusion methods do not require heuristic map post-processing"). Also directly relevant: naive product/sum pixel-wise fusion is confirmed (again) as a weak baseline, and simple disjunction is sensitive to per-detector-specific cleaning parameters.

**Limitations the authors themselves state (this is also THE key result for evaluation practice, Sec. IV-C, "Limitations of Localization Performance Measures"):**
- "currently used measures for evaluation of tampering localization schemes deliver inadequate results... often either minimal improvement or even significant deterioration of the measurable performance (both F1 and accuracy) despite clearly more precise detection" — i.e., F1/accuracy on their own can rank a visually-worse map above a visually-better one (Sec. IV-C).
- Attributes part of the problem to "collateral damage": pixels with different raw values but the same semantic content (e.g., blending halos around inserted objects) that penalize precise detectors under naive pixel-count metrics.
- States plainly in the Conclusion (Sec. V): "existing metrics... ignore spatial relationships of detected objects, and are not accurate enough to reflect differences between rough and precise object representation... need for further research towards better evaluation protocols."
- Framework limitation: "expects unified tampering probability maps as an input. Further work is required to include arbitrary detectors, possibly operating on different domains (e.g., segments or super-pixels) or incapable of probabilistic output" (Sec. V).

**Dataset note (cross-checked against paper text, see full comparison in the dataset section below):** Sec. IV-A of THIS paper describes a dataset of "120 diverse forgeries" from 3 cameras (Sony α57 personal collection; Nikon D90 and D7000 from the RAISE dataset [17]), 1920×1080 TIFF images cropped from the middle, PRNU estimated from 200 (Nikon) or 90 (Sony) flat/natural images, predictor trained on 25,000 patches from 50 images. **This is a smaller/earlier version of what later became the full "Realistic Tampering Dataset."** The dataset's own webpage (fetched separately, see below) lists this WIFS 2016 paper as one of two accompanying papers but states the full public dataset has 220 forgeries from 4 cameras (adds Canon 60D). The authoritative source for the full/current dataset is therefore korus2017_multiscale-prnu (TIFS 2017), checked next.

## korus2017_multiscale-prnu

**Full citation:** P. Korus, J. Huang, "Multi-scale Analysis Strategies in PRNU-based Tampering Localization," IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security, 2017 (venue confirmed via cross-reference: korus2016_random-fields.pdf ref. [13] cites this exact paper as "IEEE Trans. Inf. Forensics Security, To appear"; the PDF's own header/copyright line just says "Copyright (c) 2016 IEEE" with no journal name printed in the extracted text -- `not verified from retrieved text` for volume/issue/page numbers and DOI). Source code stated as available at https://github.com/pkorus/multiscale-prnu (Sec. I, p.1).
**Local file:** `references/papers/korus2017_multiscale-prnu.pdf` (16 pages), text extraction: clean

**What the method computes:**
- Baseline single-scale PRNU localization (Sec. II-A): image model y=(1+k)x+n; residual r=y-D(y)~yk+n' (Eq. 1-2, D=denoiser e.g. wavelet or BM3D); sliding-window normalized correlation q between residual and PRNU estimate (Eq. 3); tampering probability from a two-hypothesis Gaussian likelihood ratio, c=B(q|q_hat,sigma0,sigma1) (Eq. 6a-6b), with q_hat predicted from image content via a trained "correlation predictor" Q(y|theta) (handles saturated/textured regions). Standard window omega=128 (Chen et al. recommendation), central-pixel attribution, heuristic post-processing (threshold, remove small components <1/4 window area, disk dilation r=10px) -- Algorithm 1.
- **Multi-scale fusion (MSF) -- the core energy formulation for priority #2:** candidate maps {c^(s)} obtained at multiple window sizes omega_s in {32,48,64,96,128,192,256}, each with a reliability map p^(s). Energy (Eq. 8):
  E(t) = (1/S) * Sum_i Sum_s E_tau(c_i^(s), t_i) + alpha * Sum_i t_i + Sum_i Sum_{j in Xi_i} beta_ij|t_i - t_j|
  -- data term (resemblance to each scale's map) + bias term alpha (shifts decision toward tampered/authentic) + pairwise smoothness term over an 8-neighbor grid Xi_i (2nd-order neighborhood).
  - Data-term potential (Eq. 9-10, same trapezoidal-log form as the WIFS companion paper): E_tau(c,t) = -log max(Psi_min, Psi_tau(c,t)), Psi_min=0.001 floor to avoid infinite energy/label lock-in.
  - **Threshold drift** (Eq. 11): a per-pixel, per-scale quasi-threshold tau_i^(s) is nudged up/down by delta*p_i^(s-1) depending on the smaller scale's decision, i.e. small-scale evidence progressively re-biases how large-scale evidence is read; drift is clamped to [0.05, 0.95] and -- new in this paper vs. the 2016 predecessor -- modulated by each region's own reliability p (Sec. III-B) rather than applied uniformly.
  - **Reliability map** (Eq. 12): p_i = 1 - exp(-xi0*|c_i-0.5|^xi1), fit by grid search (xi0=30, xi1=2.5); reliability grows smoothly with a score's distance from 0.5 (i.e. from "undecided"). Authors explicitly tested the degenerate case p=1 (their earlier, unweighted approach) and found it gave the *worst* F1 among tested reliability curves (~0.60 vs ~0.63 best) -- direct evidence that modulating trust by cue confidence (not just using the raw score) measurably helps fusion. Only fully-empty candidate maps are discarded outright; noisy-but-informative maps are kept and down-weighted via reliability instead (Sec. III-B) -- this is the PRNU-specific analogue of Fontani's discounting.
  - **Content-adaptive pairwise term** (Eq. 13): beta_ij = beta0 + beta1*exp(-0.5*phi^-2*||y'_i-y'_j||^2_L2) -- default (content-independent) interaction beta0 plus a content-similarity term beta1 that pulls RGB-similar neighboring pixels toward the same label (phi=25, empirical).
  - Labeling solved by graph cuts (submodular since beta0,beta1>=0) via the UGM toolbox (Boykov-Kolmogorov style solver) -- Sec. V-A.
- **Adaptive-window strategy** (Sec. IV-B, Algorithm 3): starts at the smallest window (32px) per pixel location and grows the window only while the decision stays unconfident (|c-0.5|<0.5-delta_c1); stops early if a confident decision is reinforced by the next larger scale (|c-0.5|>delta_c2 and same sign) to avoid unnecessary blurring by large windows; final decision still resolved with a (simplified, single-map) CRF, Eq. 14.
- **Segmentation-guided strategy** (Sec. IV-A, Algorithm 2): computes the omega=128 correlation only over pixels within a rough RGB-distance-based local segmentation mask (Eq. 15, cleaned with 8px morphological closing, kept only the central connected component, grown if <omega_min^2 pixels); hypothesis variances sigma0,sigma1 are re-matched to the actual (usually reduced) pixel count via cubic-spline interpolation over the multi-scale camera model.
- All three strategies can use either heuristic post-processing (threshold + small-component removal, no dilation needed since edges are already followed more precisely) or the CRF-based decision (Eq. 14, a single-map special case of Eq. 8).

**Reported performance:**
- Table III (best average F1 x100, synthetic square forgeries, per camera per tampering size 48-256px): MSF strategy dominates essentially everywhere, e.g. Sony a57 at 128px tampering: single 128px-window detector 35, MSF 50; at 256px: single 128px window 63, MSF 71. Full numeric table given for 4 cameras x 6 tampering sizes x 7 single-scale windows + 4 multi-scale strategies (Sec. V-D, Table III).
- Fig. 8 histogram (Nikon D7000, 64px square tampering): MSF achieves F1>0.5 on 80/250 images vs 9/250 for the standard 128px single-scale detector -- an approximately 9x increase in the fraction of small forgeries reliably detected.
- Realistic-forgery dataset average F1 (Table IV, "chosen param." column, i.e. the actual deployed universal parameter set): Sony a57 0.671, Canon 60D 0.607, Nikon D90 0.569, Nikon D7000 0.610 -- vs. "zeroed param." (CRF terms off, i.e. plain thresholding) 0.612 / 0.539 / 0.440 / 0.537, and vs. the camera-specific "best param." upper bound 0.677 / 0.627 / 0.571 / 0.638 (Sec. V-E). So a single universal parameter configuration recovers nearly all of the camera-specific-tuned ceiling, and CRF terms alone are worth roughly +0.03 to +0.13 F1 over a bare thresholded map.
- Robustness to JPEG recompression (Sec. V-F, Fig. 12; 136 realistic images x 6 JPEG qualities 75-100 + original TIFF): MSF "delivers the best results with consistent benefits for all JPEG quality levels," while single-scale small windows (32-64px) become "no longer useful below quality 90-95."
- Qualitative failure cases explicitly named: segmentation-guided strategy deteriorates on "highly detailed objects with small and dark areas of irregular shape" and "irregularly shaped saturated areas" (Sec. V-E); MSF itself had "only a few cases with considerably worse results" (three named Canon 60D images) traced to "confident false positives in medium scale candidate maps... in highly-textured areas of low or medium brightness... attributed to inadequate performance of the correlation predictor" (Sec. V-E).

**Relevance to our fusion and decision layer:** This is the primary source for a concrete, reimplementable energy-based fusion formulation (Eq. 8-13): data term (per-cue, per-scale calibrated potential with a floor against infinite energy) + bias term (global prior toward tampered/authentic, tunable) + content-adaptive pairwise smoothness term, solved by graph cuts. It directly generalizes to our multi-cue (not just multi-scale) setting: replace "scale s" with "cue c" and the same Eq. 8 becomes a genuine multi-cue CRF fusion, which is a substantial upgrade over our current fixed-weight sum + single global threshold + separate morphological cleanup. The **reliability-map mechanism (Eq. 12)** is a second, complementary answer to the "cue unavailable/weak" problem named in our brief: instead of Fontani's discounting-to-doubt at the evidence-combination stage, Korus modulates how strongly a cue's decision propagates spatially (via the threshold-drift weighting) by a smooth, score-confidence-based reliability curve -- and the paper's own ablation (p=1 is the worst-performing configuration tested) is direct evidence this matters, not just a theoretical nicety. The explicit warning that F1/accuracy do not reward more-precise-but-differently-shaped detections (echoed from the WIFS paper) reinforces that we should not tune purely against mean Dice at one operating point.

**Limitations the authors themselves state:**
- Segmentation-guided strategy: "it remains an open problem to generate reliable predictions for irregular image segments" and it "clearly suffered in more challenging conditions" -- scattered/deteriorated performance for highly detailed or saturated irregular regions (Sec. V-E, Sec. VI).
- "despite explicit care for potentially unreliable regions in the predictor, confident false positives still occur. At the moment, it may be beneficial to use a separate detector and either cross-reference the results or include this information directly in the localization algorithm" (Sec. VI) -- i.e. the authors themselves flag that their own reliability model is imperfect and gesture toward exactly the kind of external cue-reliability fusion (Fontani-style) that we are trying to build.
- Parameter selection is acknowledged as heuristic/approximate: exact optimization is "intractable... due to combinatorial complexity of the partition function of the random field," and available approximations (saddle-point likelihood) "could not guarantee convergence to optimal parameters" -- the paper resorts to random search plus a fixed "universal" parameter set rather than a principled per-image calibration (Sec. V-C).
- Note 2 on p.10: even for object-insertion forgeries "the actual ground truth mask typically does not follow exactly the edges of the object... needed some post-processing to make the forgery more visually appealing" -- an explicit caveat about ground-truth mask fidelity that matters for our evaluation section.
- ROC curves were not the explicit optimization target ("our parameter selection procedure did not involve explicit optimization of ROC performance," Sec. V-D) -- reported ROC/F1 numbers should be read as "what this F1-tuned configuration also achieves on ROC," not as independently-optimized ROC performance.

## korus2016_multiscale-fusion

**Full citation:** P. Korus, J. Huang, "Multi-Scale Fusion for Improved Localization of Malicious Tampering in Digital Images," IEEE Transactions on Image Processing, vol. 25, no. 3, pp. 1312-1326, 2016 (volume/issue/pages cross-confirmed via korus2017_multiscale-prnu.pdf reference [12] and korus2016_random-fields.pdf reference [16], both of which cite this exact title/venue/vol/no/year; the PDF's own extracted text shows "Copyright (c) 2016 IEEE" but no journal name or page numbers in-line -- `not verified from retrieved text` for DOI).
**Local file:** `references/papers/korus2016_multiscale-fusion.pdf` (15 pages), text extraction: clean (pypdf logged benign "Ignoring wrong pointing object" xref warnings during parsing, but page text came through intact and readable).

**What the method computes:**
- This is the original paper establishing the energy-minimization multi-scale fusion framework later adapted for PRNU in korus2017_multiscale-prnu (that paper's Eq. 5/8 is a direct descendant of this paper's Eq. 6). Application domain here is JPEG double-compression splicing detection via mode-based first digit features (MBFDF, Sec. II-B), not PRNU.
- Formal fusion problem (Sec. III-A): starts from a Bayesian MAP formulation, argmax_t P(c^(s):s|t)P(t) (Eq. 2), assumes per-unit and per-scale conditional independence to get Eq. 4, and models the prior P(t) as an MRF (Ising model, Sec. III-A) with Gibbs potentials, converting to energy minimization by taking -log. Resulting energy (Eq. 6):
  (1/S) * Sum_i Sum_s E_tau(c_i^(s), t_i) + alpha * Sum_i t_i + beta * Sum_i Sum_{j in Xi_i} |t_i - t_j|
  -- structurally identical to the later PRNU paper's Eq. 8 (this is the "original algorithm" referenced there), but here beta is a single scalar (content-independent) rather than the content-adaptive beta_ij introduced later; footnote 1 (p.6) explicitly flags this as future work: "interaction strength beta could be chosen adaptively, e.g., based on the actual image content... We leave this aspect for our future work" -- i.e. the content-adaptive pairwise term in the later paper directly answers a gap this paper names itself.
  - Same data-term construction as later papers: E_tau(c,t) = -log max(Psi_min, Psi_tau(c,t)) (Eq. 7-8), Psi_min=0.001 floor, tau a quasi-threshold.
  - Same threshold-drift mechanism (Eq. 9), first introduced here: tau_i^(s) is carried over from smaller scales and nudged by delta based on whether the smaller scale's score was above/below its own threshold; unreliable regions (p_i^(s) <= p_th) freeze the threshold (no drift) rather than being weighted, which is a cruder mechanism than the smooth reliability-weighting introduced later.
  - **Unreliable-region handling (Sec. II-E, directly relevant to priority #1):** each candidate map has a reliability map p^(s); regions below a reliability threshold p_th are treated as unreliable. Whole candidate maps can also be discarded outright if their score distribution resembles Gaussian noise -- detected by fitting a Gaussian (mu, sigma) via MLE to the map's reliable-region scores and rejecting "meaningless low-variance noise" (mu=0.5+/-0.05, sigma<0.05) or "empty meaningless maps" (|0.5-mu|>0.4, sigma<0.05); surviving maps are further screened by a KL-divergence test against their own Gaussian fit (threshold theta_KL, chosen empirically to maximize F1, Sec. IV-B). This is a coarser, binary (keep/discard whole map) precursor to the smooth, per-pixel reliability curve of the 2017 PRNU paper.
  - **Explicit alternative handling of unreliable regions within EM fusion (Sec. III-A, Fig. 8):** unreliable candidate scores are replaced by a constant c_sat before being fed into the energy; c_sat=0 is called "the mentioned conservative approach" (labels unreliable regions as authentic by default) but the solver can still overturn this via neighborhood propagation from reliable neighbors; c_sat near tau instead "introduces more ambiguity and makes it easier for the solver to propagate reliable scores from the neighborhood into unreliable regions." Fig. 8 shows a worked example: F1 = 0.764 (beta=0, c_sat=0) vs 0.746 (beta=2.5, c_sat=0) vs 0.837 (beta=2.5, c_sat=0.5) -- i.e. combining nonzero smoothness with a "don't know" (near-threshold) fill value for unreliable pixels outperforms either a hard authentic default or smoothness alone. Authors caution c_sat should ideally be chosen per-image and that blindly setting c_sat near tau risks propagating misidentified-unreliable (actually-wrong) regions, since "saturated regions may produce highly confident incorrect decisions" (Sec. III-A).
  - Two additional heuristic (non-CRF) fusion strategies are proposed and evaluated: **bottom-up (BU)** fusion, which starts from the smallest-scale candidate map and successively validates/removes connected components against larger scales; and **top-down (TD)** fusion, which starts from the largest scale and refines shapes using smaller scales (Sec. III-B, Algorithms visualized in Fig. 9-10). Both use K-means (2 centroids) to binarize each candidate map first, discard unreliable maps, remove small connected components, then iteratively reconcile shapes across scales; final scores are a weighted sum of candidate scores in agreed regions (weights w=[128,64,32,16,8,4,2,1]/255, favoring smaller/finer scales).
  - Baselines used for comparison (Sec. III-C): majority voting (MV, decision-level), simple averaging (AV, measurement-level, equivalent to EM with alpha=beta=delta=0), an SVM-based supervised fusion (SL, features = candidate scores + reliability scores + neighbor scores + local averages), and K-means clustering fusion (CA, unsupervised, per-pixel S-dimensional feature vector of all scale scores).

**Reported performance:**
- Primary metric: F1 = 2TP/(2TP+FN+FP) (Eq. 10), explicitly justified as "more representative than accuracy for tampering-localization" (Sec. IV, no further quantitative argument given here -- this exact claim is elaborated with evidence in the companion WIFS 2016 paper). ROC curves also reported "for the sake of discussion completeness" but not used to drive parameter choices.
- Synthetic dataset (Sec. IV-A): 32,000 multi-scale candidate map sets from 200 uncompressed BOSSbase images, 8 tampering shape patterns (Fig. 11) x 2 scenarios (double-inside/single-inside) x random Q1/Q2 JPEG pairs; 24,000 sets used for evaluation after filtering unreliable sets, remainder for SVM training / EM parameter search.
- Best average F1 (x100, Fig. 14, averaged over all patterns): double-inside scenario -- best individual scale (32px) ~59.24, EM 76.74, EM' (general params) 76.03, TD 77.35, BU 78.63 (best), SL 76.13, CA 75.75; single-inside scenario -- best individual scale ~59.02, EM 81.88, TD 80.31 (approx), BU 80.17, SL 78.75. "BU fusion delivered the best results for 7 out of 8 patterns in the double-inside scenario"; "In the single-inside scenario the best results for 6 patterns were delivered by the EM fusion" (Sec. IV-C). MV and AV fusion were markedly worse and "unsuitable" for complex shapes (Sec. IV-C).
- Realistic dataset (Sec. IV-D, Table II; 48 uncompressed images from a copy-move benchmark [Christlein et al.], resized to 800px width, Q1=80/Q2=90): average F1 -- best single scale (32px) 85.5, oracle (best-scale-per-image) 87.0, EM' 85.4, TD 87.6 (best), BU 87.5, MV 87.0(=oracle tie per table columns, "Oracle" and "MV" are separate columns -- MV listed at 87.0), AV 84.2, SL 85.6, SL' 82.9, CA 74.3. Notably: "the proposed EM and BU fusion strategies were slightly better (F1=0.876 for EM', F1=0.875 for BU) than the oracle" (F1=0.870) that always picks the single best-performing individual scale per image -- i.e. fusion beat even a hypothetical best-possible single-scale choice (Sec. IV-D). A fixed 64px window (the setting "recommended by Amerini et al.") only reached F1=0.733.
- Runtime (Table II, Sec. V-A, Matlab + C/C++ MEX on a 3.6GHz Core i7-4790): decision fusion time per image: MV/AV ~0.004s, EM ~0.004s, TD 0.015s, BU 0.05s, SL 3.03s (slow, sensitive to training-set size), SL' 0.22s (smaller/faster SVM, accuracy penalty), CA 0.01s. Sliding-window feature extraction itself (3.8s average per image) dominates total runtime, not fusion.

**Relevance to our fusion and decision layer:** This paper is the origin of the exact energy formulation (Eq. 6-9) that the later PRNU paper generalizes with content-adaptive pairwise terms and continuous reliability weighting — reading both together shows the lineage: crude whole-map rejection + hard unreliable-region freezing (this paper) evolved into smooth per-pixel reliability curves and content-adaptive neighborhood interactions (2017 paper) precisely because the cruder mechanisms left gaps the authors flag themselves (footnote 1, Sec. V-B). The c_sat mechanism (Fig. 8, Sec. III-A) is a second concrete design pattern for handling an unreliable/unavailable cue in a spatial fusion setting: instead of Fontani's mass-to-doubt at the evidence level, here an unreliable region's raw score is replaced with a fill value close to the decision threshold (i.e., "assume ambiguous, let neighbors decide"), which is complementary and could combine with Fontani-style discounting in a multi-cue-multi-scale CRF for our system. Also directly useful: the empirical finding that fusion can beat even an oracle that always picks the best single detector per image (Sec. IV-D) is a strong argument for building a real fusion mechanism rather than trying to pick a single "best" cue per image.

**Limitations the authors themselves state:**
- The pairwise interaction strength beta is content-independent (constant) in this paper; adapting it to image content is explicitly deferred: "we leave this aspect for our future work" (footnote 1, p.6) -- resolved in the 2017 PRNU follow-up.
- SVM-based (SL) fusion "is ultimately crippled by the lack of flexibility... would require separate training for every possible combination of valid inputs, the number of which grows very quickly (255 in this study)" (Sec. V-B) -- a direct, quantified statement of the combinatorial-training problem that also motivated Fontani's DST framework.
- "Despite including neighborhood-related features, the decisions for every authentication unit are still independent [in the SL/CA baselines]. This issue could possibly be addressed by adopting Markov-like dependencies... but this issue requires separate future research" (Sec. V-B).
- "If the tampered regions differ in size or shape considerably, it might be beneficial to adapt the parameters... to local image characteristics, or even to directly exploit image content" (Sec. V-B) -- again, resolved partially by the later paper's content-adaptive beta_ij.
- Parameter selection (alpha, beta, delta for EM fusion) was done via grid search separately for each of 8 tampering-shape patterns and needed to be split into "irregular shapes" vs. "solid shapes" groups because a single global parameter set underperformed pattern-specific tuning (Table I, Sec. IV-B) -- an implicit admission that a single fixed parameter set is a compromise, not optimal.
- Explicitly scoped to a single forensic feature (MBFDF/JPEG double compression) and leaves generalization to other detectors (e.g. PRNU) and truly multi-modal (not just multi-scale) fusion as future work (Sec. VI Conclusions): "We will also investigate suitability of multi-scale fusion for other forensic features... Positive results would indicate feasibility of a combined multi-scale and multi-modal approach" -- i.e. at the time of this paper, multi-cue (as opposed to multi-scale) fusion of this kind was explicitly unproven.

Explicitly states: "In tampering localization, decision fusion is still an open problem, and most of the reported systems rely on naive pixel-wise application of simple combination rules, or simple heuristics for candidate map selection" and repeats the finding (citing Cozzolino et al.) that "standard product fusion and simple map summation deliver best results among a variety of pixel-wise combination rules" -- but immediately qualifies: "due to possible multi-scale effects in multi-clue detectors... pixel-wise fusion is clearly sub-optimal," pointing to content-adaptive CRFs (citing the Korus PRNU paper) as "a promising approach" that nonetheless still needs "further work."
- Fig. 11 in the survey shows a concrete example: CFA + PRNU pixel-wise fusion (sum, product, empirical) on a copy-move (person duplication on an escalator) forgery, illustrating that "even naive pixel-wise combination rules allow to improve upon individual candidate probability maps" -- i.e. the survey's own visual evidence supports naive fusion as a working baseline, just not an optimal one.

**Relevance to our fusion and decision layer:** This survey is the strongest available third-party validation that (a) naive pixel-wise fusion (sum/product) is a recognized, literature-wide weak baseline, not a strawman specific to the Korus fusion papers -- "most of the reported systems rely on naive pixel-wise application of simple combination rules" (Sec. 4.12); (b) DSTE/fuzzy-logic measurement-level fusion is the recognized "sophisticated" alternative specifically because it handles interim-decision compatibility and uncertainty systematically, which is exactly the gap in our current fixed-weight-sum design; (c) CRF-based, content-adaptive spatial fusion is identified as the current promising direction for localization specifically (as opposed to whole-image detection), again citing the same Korus energy-formulation papers already logged above. Existing studies adopt simple pixel-wise measures like true/false positive/negative rates, precision, recall, accuracy, or F1 measure. **What the method computes:** This is a **supervised, learned** (not training-free) deep-learning fusion of five hand-crafted forensic localization algorithms' output maps -- included here because it is the most directly comparable learned-fusion baseline in our reading set, useful for the training-free-vs-learned expectation band (priority #5).
- Five input detectors, fixed by matching a prior comparison paper's selection (Sec. - Two fusion architectures compared (Sec. III): (a) **Eff-B4-Unet**, a U-Net variant using an EfficientNet-B4 encoder; (b) **OwAF** (Operation-wise Attention Fusion), adapted from an image-restoration architecture (Suganuma et al., ref. [25]): stacks of "operation-wise attention" layers, each running several parallel operations (1x1/3x3/5x5/7x7 convolutions, 3x3 average and max pooling) weighted by a learned attention mechanism that selects which operation matters most for a given input; adapted here to 4 stacked layers (reduced from the original 10, "to avoid overfitting"), one operation layer per attention layer (vs. 4 in the original), and dilated convolutions replaced by simple convolutions (found to work better in preliminary experiments) -- both changes are pragmatic anti-overfitting/architecture-fit adjustments the authors made themselves, not off-the-shelf reuse.

Trained on the **DEFACTO** dataset (150,000+ synthetic-forgery images with ground truth; splicing/copy-move/object-removal/face-morphing types, only the first three used here), with 15,000 images for training, 1,000 for validation (300 used per epoch for speed), 1,000 held out for in-distribution testing.
- Evaluation metrics (Sec. IV-A): macro-averaged F1, F1 for the tampered class specifically, and average IoU, all at a fixed binarization threshold of 0.5 (explicitly stated per table).

**Reported performance:**
- **In-distribution (DEFACTO test set, Table I, n=1000, threshold 0.5):** individual detectors are weak -- BLK (macro-F1 0.463, F1-tampered 0.090, IoU 0.053), ADQ1 (0.573, 0.221, 0.123, the best individual detector here), CAGI (0.479, 0.142, 0.072), DCT (0.509, 0.186, 0.101), Splicebuster (0.554, 0.153, 0.087). Both fusion networks are dramatically better: Eff-B4-Unet (0.908, 0.798, 0.690), OwAF (0.912, 0.829, 0.707, the best overall). The paper explicitly attributes low individual-detector scores partly to the fixed 0.5 threshold ("many algorithms might operate on lower thresholds") and notes "averaging the results from many binarization ranges did not significantly improve the evaluation results" for the individual methods regardless.
- **Cross-dataset generalization (Table II, threshold 0.5) -- the key result for our expectation-band question:** on **CASIA v2** (5,123 images, ground truth from a third paper's semi-automated relabeling of 2,195 of them): individual detectors score similarly to or slightly better than on DEFACTO (ADQ1 0.573/0.217/0.130, Splicebuster 0.576/0.163/0.093, etc.); a competing non-deep fusion method (Iakovidou et al., "Knowledge-based fusion for image tampering localization," IFIP AIAI 2020) scores 0.598/0.253/0.166; OwAF still wins at 0.611/0.270/0.172 -- **but OwAF's own tampered-class F1 collapses from 0.829 (in-distribution, DEFACTO) to 0.270 (out-of-distribution, CASIA v2) and IoU from 0.707 to 0.172, a roughly 67-76% relative drop**, despite still edging out the alternatives. On the **First IFS-TC Image Forensics Challenge** dataset (306 splicing-only cases): individual detector scores drop further across the board, EXCEPT Splicebuster, which *improves* to 0.560/0.199/0.129 and **outperforms both fusion methods** on this dataset; Iakovidou et al.'s fusion scores 0.549/0.171/0.112; OwAF actually scores worst of the three (0.529/0.140/0.106) -- i.e. the learned fusion network, trained on DEFACTO, loses to a single 2015-era blind (training-free) detector when evaluated on a dataset whose manipulation style differs enough from its training distribution.
- The authors' own diagnosis (Sec. IV-B): "our trained models have overfitted to the training set manipulations... possibly learned to localize specific forgeries, like shapes and patterns from the outputs of individual algorithms that frequently appear in the DEFACTO dataset" (e.g. DEFACTO's synthetic objects: traffic signs, birds, airplanes, humans), and on IFS-TC specifically: "our fusion model learned to focus more on the individual localization maps that achieved better performance in the training set, namely ADQ1 and DCT... [but] the best performing individual algorithm is Splicebuster and this possibly justifies the poor performance of OwAF."

**Relevance to our fusion and decision layer:** This paper is the clearest concrete evidence in our reading set of the risk of a *learned* fusion stage: it can achieve very large apparent gains over individual training-free detectors in-distribution (IoU roughly 0.05-0.12 individually to 0.71 fused) yet its advantage is not robust -- on a dataset whose forgery style differs from training, the same learned fusion network can fall *below* a single training-free detector (Splicebuster on IFS-TC) and well below its own in-distribution numbers. This is direct, quantified support for a training-free fusion design philosophy: a fusion mechanism whose combining logic is not itself fit to a specific training distribution (Fontani's DST discounting, or Korus's CRF energy with hand-set/lightly-tuned parameters) does not carry this particular failure mode, though it has its own separate robustness limits (cf. Verdoliva's point about brittle statistical assumptions). It's also a useful reminder that "our fusion beats naive summation on our benchmark" is a claim that needs cross-dataset (or at least cross-scene/cross-camera) testing to be trustworthy, not just in-sample improvement.

**Limitations the authors themselves state:**
- "An important limitation of this work is the generalization ability of the fusion model and stems from the general limitations of the underlying supervised learning scheme and the insufficient representativeness of the dataset that was used for training" (Sec. V, Conclusions) -- a direct, self-diagnosed statement that the fusion stage's weakness is inherent to supervised learning on one dataset, not incidental.
- "The low evaluation performance of individual models is a major indication that the forgery localization problem is very difficult and is even more challenging to design a general fusion solution for images in the wild. These observations are also reported by other researchers [Zampoglou, Papadopoulos & Kompatsiaris 2017, the same large-scale web-image evaluation cited by korus2017_integrity-survey and verdoliva2020]" (Sec. IV-B) -- an explicit acknowledgment that the field-wide difficulty of localization (not just this paper's specific fusion choice) is the deeper obstacle.
- Named future-work items to address generalization: enlarging/diversifying the training dataset with other synthetic sources, task-specific regularization (e.g. feature map dropout), and multi-stream architectures that also look at the raw input image rather than only the detector output maps (Sec. V) -- i.e. the authors' own prescribed fix is more data and regularization, not a change of paradigm away from supervised learning.

## Evaluation practice in this literature

- **Metrics used.** F1 = 2TP/(2TP+FN+FP) is the dominant metric across the Korus papers, explicitly preferred over raw accuracy for localization (`korus2016_multiscale-fusion.pdf` Sec. IV: "more representative than accuracy for tampering-localization," no further justification given in that paper). ROC curves / AUC are reported "for the sake of discussion completeness" in the Korus multi-scale papers but explicitly NOT used to drive parameter selection (`korus2017_multiscale-prnu.pdf` Sec. V-D: "our parameter selection procedure did not involve explicit optimization of ROC performance"). Fontani's classification-level work (not per-pixel localization) uses AUC over a restricted false-alarm range (PFA in [0,0.3]) because training targeted overall accuracy, not ROC shape (`fontani2013_dempster-shafer.pdf` Sec. IV-B). `charitidis2021_fusion-attention.pdf` (Sec. IV-A) uses macro-F1, F1 restricted to the tampered class, and IoU, all at one fixed binarization threshold (0.5) -- and explicitly finds that macro-F1 systematically overstates performance relative to F1(tampered): "the macro-averaged F1 score is higher compared to the F1 score for the tampered class for all evaluations. This means that the models can predict the pristine regions more accurately than the tampered ones" (Sec. IV-B) -- a concrete warning that an aggregate/macro metric can hide poor tampered-region detection, directly relevant to any decision to report a single Dice/F1 number.
- **Fixed-threshold vs. threshold-free.** Both conventions appear, sometimes in the same paper. The Korus multi-scale papers report BOTH "average F1" (F1 at one threshold, averaged over images -- i.e. what a real deployed system would see) AND "peak F1" (per-image best-achievable F1 over all thresholds, then averaged -- an oracle upper bound), and are careful to label which is which (`korus2017_multiscale-prnu.pdf` Sec. V, e.g. Fig. 8 "histograms of peak F1 scores"). `korus2016_multiscale-fusion.pdf` Sec. IV-C explicitly notes it reports "the highest achievable F1 score over different thresholds... which serves as an upper bound on the tampering localization capability" for the synthetic-forgery comparison, but chooses a single threshold per image for the realistic-forgery comparison (Sec. IV-D). `charitidis2021_fusion-attention.pdf` reports only a single fixed threshold (0.5) throughout, with no oracle/peak alternative -- the most single-operating-point-only evaluation among these papers.
- **Whether authentic (pristine) images are included.** Fontani's classification-level dataset explicitly balances tampered and pristine images (4800/4800 synthetic, 70/70 realistic) since it is solving image-level binary classification (`fontani2013_dempster-shafer.pdf` Sec. IV-A3). The Korus localization papers instead evaluate per-pixel within each tampered image (foreground/tampered pixels vs. background/authentic pixels of the SAME image), even though the underlying dataset also contains a pristine counterpart of every tampered image (220 pairs) -- none of the Korus papers we read describe a separate false-positive-rate test on wholly-pristine images as part of the localization evaluation protocol; this is a gap `not verified from retrieved text` as being addressed anywhere in these papers.
- **Aggregation across images.** All papers average per-image scores (mean F1 / mean IoU / mean AUC across the test set) rather than pooling pixels/detections globally across the whole test set before computing one metric -- this matters because per-image averaging weights every image equally regardless of its tampered-region size, while pooled/micro-averaging would weight large forgeries more.
- **Confidence intervals / uncertainty.** No paper in this set reports formal statistical confidence intervals on its headline numbers. The closest analogs: `fontani2013_dempster-shafer.pdf` Fig. 6-7 plots max/min uncertainty bars across 20 repeated train/test splits for methods requiring training (SVM, OR-based fusion), and separately reports a robustness experiment (AUC std. dev. of 0.02 under Gaussian-perturbed tool reliabilities, Sec. IV-B); `korus2016_multiscale-fusion.pdf` Fig. 13 similarly plots per-fold min/max ROC ranges over train/test repetitions; `korus2017_multiscale-prnu.pdf` Table IV reports a 3-way sensitivity comparison (zeroed / chosen-universal / per-camera-best parameters) as an implicit range rather than a CI. None of these are confidence intervals in the classical statistical sense (no reported standard errors on the mean F1/Dice across the test set itself).
- **Explicit warnings that a metric can mislead.** This is the strongest and most repeated theme across the set. `korus2016_random-fields.pdf` Sec. IV-C (title: "Limitations of Localization Performance Measures") shows a worked example where a visually more precise, content-guided CRF localization scores WORSE on F1/accuracy than a cruder, content-independent one (F1 0.664 vs. 0.812 vs. 0.844 for pixel-wise product fusion, on the same forgery, Fig. 6) and concludes "currently used measures for evaluation of tampering localization schemes deliver inadequate results." `korus2017_integrity-survey.pdf` (Conclusion, p.27) elevates this to a field-wide claim: "There are no widely accepted evaluation protocols or performance measures and the existing ones may poorly reflect on the actual utility of the localization results... Such metrics are inherently sub-optimal as they ignore spatial relationships in the localization map." `charitidis2021_fusion-attention.pdf` adds the macro-F1-vs-tampered-F1 divergence noted above as a second, independent instance of the same general problem (aggregate metrics can look good while missing what matters).

## Honest expectation band for training-free methods

Collecting every hand-crafted-cue and hand-crafted-fusion number actually read this session (all quoted with location; no numbers invented):

- **Individual training-free cues, per-pixel localization, on harder/more heterogeneous benchmarks (CASIA v2, IFS-TC, DEFACTO):** macro-F1 roughly 0.46-0.58, F1 restricted to the tampered class roughly 0.09-0.22, IoU roughly 0.05-0.13 (best individual detector across all three of `charitidis2021_fusion-attention.pdf`'s tables was ADQ1 at IoU 0.123-0.130, or Splicebuster on IFS-TC at IoU 0.129) -- `charitidis2021_fusion-attention.pdf` Table I and Table II.
- **Individual training-free cues on the more controlled Korus realistic-tampering setting:** max F1 CFA detector 0.44, PRNU detector 0.49 (`korus2016_random-fields.pdf` Fig. 2 caption table); best single-scale PRNU window F1 in the range ~0.44-0.61 depending on camera and window size (`korus2017_multiscale-prnu.pdf` Table IV "zeroed param." column, 0.440-0.612).
- **Naive/pixel-wise fusion (sum, product, disjunction) of 2+ training-free cues:** max F1 roughly 0.57-0.61 on the Korus WIFS realistic set (`korus2016_random-fields.pdf` Fig. 2 caption table); this is the exact "naive weighted-sum" family our current pipeline resembles.
- **Well-designed training-free fusion (CRF-based spatial fusion, or DST-based evidence fusion):** F1 roughly 0.67-0.69 for CRF pixel fusion of CFA+PRNU (`korus2016_random-fields.pdf`); average F1 0.57-0.68 for CRF-based multi-scale PRNU fusion across 4 cameras with a single universal parameter set (`korus2017_multiscale-prnu.pdf` Table IV "chosen param." column); F1 roughly 0.876-0.876 (EM'/BU fusion) for MBFDF-based multi-scale fusion on a small, favorably-compressed realistic set (`korus2016_multiscale-fusion.pdf` Table II) -- notably this last number is higher than the PRNU-based fusion numbers, `INFERENCE:` likely because the realistic test images there were compressed at high, well-separated JPEG quality factors (Q1=80/Q2=90) that are unusually favorable for JPEG-based cues, not because JPEG-based fusion is intrinsically stronger than PRNU-based fusion in general; AUC 0.745 for Fontani's DST-fused classification (not per-pixel localization) on a small realistic image-level test set (`fontani2013_dempster-shafer.pdf` Fig. 7a).
- **Supervised/learned fusion of the same training-free cues, in-distribution:** IoU up to 0.707, F1(tampered) up to 0.829 (`charitidis2021_fusion-attention.pdf` Table I, OwAF on the DEFACTO test split it was trained on) -- substantially higher than any training-free fusion number above, but see the next point.
- **The same learned fusion, out-of-distribution:** IoU collapses to 0.172 (CASIA v2) and 0.106 (IFS-TC, where it is actually beaten by a single training-free detector, Splicebuster, at IoU 0.129) (`charitidis2021_fusion-attention.pdf` Table II) -- i.e. the apparent large advantage of learned fusion is not robust across datasets.
- **Supervised handcrafted-feature classifiers (trained, but still hand-crafted features) vs. deep networks, under compression stress (deepfake video detection, not image tampering localization, but the clearest quantified handcrafted-vs-learned comparison available):** accuracy 99.03% (handcrafted, uncompressed) down to 65.58% (handcrafted, low-quality-compressed) vs. 99.59% down to 94.28% (very deep network) -- a gap that "is about 15%... and 15% more using a very deep network" specifically under strong compression, while the two are nearly tied (99.03% vs 99.59%) in ideal/uncompressed conditions (`verdoliva2020_deepfakes.pdf` Table I and Sec. VI text).
- **Supervised handcrafted-feature detection under train/test misalignment:** ~94% accuracy with aligned train/test (2013 IEEE IFS-TC challenge) collapsing to "sometimes close to 50%, that is, random guess" when training and test sets are drawn from unrelated data (`verdoliva2020_deepfakes.pdf` Sec. III-D).
- **Field-level real-world caution:** "a recent evaluation on Web images has demonstrated unreliable performance of existing detectors... in uncontrolled conditions," and a large-scale NIST/DARPA MediFor evaluation found "conventional acquisition-based traces delivered poor performance" (`korus2017_integrity-survey.pdf` Conclusion, p.25-26, both citing Zampoglou et al. 2017 and Brogan et al. 2017 respectively; no specific numeric scores given in the extracted text for either).

`INFERENCE:` Taken together, a defensible expectation band for a training-free, CPU-only, hand-crafted-cue fusion system on a Korus-style realistic-tampering benchmark is roughly **F1/Dice in the 0.5-0.7 range at a single well-chosen global threshold**, with naive fixed-weight-sum fusion sitting near the bottom of that range (~0.55-0.61) and a genuinely reliability-aware, spatially-coherent fusion (CRF and/or DST-style) plausibly reaching the top of it (~0.67-0.69), based on the closest published analogs read this session. Numbers below F1 ~0.5 on harder/more diverse benchmarks (CASIA/IFS-TC/DEFACTO-style) are also consistent with this literature and should not be treated as a sign of a broken implementation. Expecting a training-free system to match a well-trained, in-distribution deep-learning fusion (IoU/F1 approaching 0.7-0.83 per `charitidis2021_fusion-attention.pdf`) is optimistic and not supported by anything read this session; expecting it to be more stable than such a system when evaluated out-of-distribution IS supported, but no paper here quantifies training-free out-of-distribution robustness with numbers directly comparable to charitidis2021's OOD collapse figures, so that specific comparison is `not verified from retrieved text` and stated here only as an inference from the separate pieces of evidence above.
