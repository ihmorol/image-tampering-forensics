## wen2016

**Full citation:** Bihan Wen, Ye Zhu, Ramanathan Subramanian, Tian-Tsong Ng, Xuanjing Shen, Stefan Winkler, "COVERAGE - A Novel Database for Copy-Move Forgery Detection", IEEE International Conference on Image Processing (ICIP), 2016. DOI not printed in the retrieved PDF.
**Retrieval:** Local PDF `references/papers/wen2016_coverage.pdf` (5 pp), extraction clean.

**What the method computes:** This is a *database* paper, not a detector. Contributions, all from Sections 2-3:
- 100 original-forged image pairs (Sec. 1 and Table 1). Originals shot on an "Iphone 6 front camera" (Sec. 2), a region of interest containing at least two similar-but-genuine objects (SGOs) cropped from each, stored lossless TIFF; forgeries made in Photoshop CS4, also lossless TIFF (Sec. 2).
- Average image size given in Table 1 as 400 x 486 for COVERAGE, versus CoMoFoD 512 x 512, Manipulation 2305 x 3020, GRIP 1024 x 768. INFERENCE: the two-line layout of the "Average Image Size" row means the first line is width and second is height; the pairing itself is unambiguous but which of the two is width is my reading, not stated.
- Six tampering types (Sec. 2): translation; scaling by factor phi; rotation clockwise by theta; free-form (free transform); illumination change; combination. Types i-iii are labelled Simple, iv-vi Complex.
- Three forgery-quality metrics (Sec. 3): SSIM and PSNR as global similarity; fPSNR (Eq. 1), a PSNR restricted to the forged-region pixel set C_f, described as "invariant to forged region size" (Sec. 3.2); and FEA (Forgery Edge Abnormality), Eq. 2-3, the difference E_f - E_o of normalised union-of-transforms sparse-modelling errors computed on patches centred on the boundary B between C_f and its complement, with two learned unitary sparsifying transforms W_f (forged region) and W_o (background), each patch choosing whichever transform gives the smaller error (Sec. 3.3, problem P1).

**How the final mask is built:** Not applicable - no detector is proposed. The masks here are hand annotations made in Photoshop, not algorithm output (Sec. 2: "Masks corresponding to the duplicated and forged regions are annotated for all image pairs").

**Source vs destination convention:** This is the single most useful thing in the paper for us, and it is stated explicitly. COVERAGE ships **two separate masks per pair**: the duplicated (source) region and the forged (pasted) region, "respectively highlighted in green and red" (Fig. 1 caption). Sec. 1 contrasts this with the others: "CoMoFod and GRIP combine the duplicated and forged region masks in a single image without demarcation. COVERAGE explicitly specifies the duplicated and forged region masks". Table 1's "Mask" row confirms: CoMoFoD = comb, GRIP = comb, COVERAGE = both forged and duplicated ticked; the Manipulation dataset's row reads as forged-mask ticked. INFERENCE: reading Table 1's column alignment from the mangled text extraction, Manipulation appears to have a forged mask but no separate duplicated mask; treat that one cell as low-confidence. Terminology used consistently in Sec. 1: "copying an object from the duplicated region of the original ... and pasting it onto the forged region within the same image" - so *duplicated = source*, *forged = destination*.

**Computational cost:** not verified from retrieved text.

**Reported performance:** Detection here is per-image *pair discrimination* accuracy, not localisation. Methodology: "We follow the original/forged image identification methodology in [1]" (Fridrich et al. 2003), Sec. 3.1; Sec. 4 says one image was randomly selected from each pair for testing.
- Table 3, COVERAGE, All (100): SIFT 50.5%, SURF 58.6%, Dense-Field (Cozzolino 2015) 71.8%, average 60.3%.
- Table 3, other datasets: CoMoFoD (200) SIFT 77.0 / SURF 51.5 / Dense-Field 72.0 / avg 66.8; Manipulate (48) 75.0 / 58.3 / 95.8 / 76.4; GRIP (100) 71.0 / 52.0 / 82.0 / 68.3.
- Worst COVERAGE cell for dense-field is Combination tampering at 50.0% (Table 3), i.e. chance.
- Table 2: overall COVERAGE SSIM 0.94, PSNR 24.8, fPSNR 14.0, FEA 26.2%, human (VP) 69.9%, CV 60.3%. FEA correlates with human accuracy at rho = 0.89, p < 0.01 (Sec. 4); fPSNR correlates rho = -0.33 (Sec. 4).

**Relevance to replacing our SIFT+convex-hull detector:** Two things. First, it independently ranks the candidate replacement above SIFT on every dataset in Table 3 - dense-field beats SIFT on COVERAGE (71.8 vs 50.5), Manipulate (95.8 vs 75.0) and GRIP (82.0 vs 71.0), and only loses on CoMoFoD (72.0 vs 77.0). Second, it forces us to decide the source/destination question before we score anything: if a dataset's ground truth is a *combined* mask (CoMoFoD, GRIP per Sec. 1) then a detector that outputs only the pasted region is penalised on half the ground-truth area, and vice versa. Fig. 3 also shows the exact failure our detector has: on a COVERAGE original with SGOs, both SIFT and dense-field produce *more* matches between the two genuine objects than between the real duplicated/forged pair (Sec. 4), so match count alone is not evidence.

**Limitations the authors themselves state:** Sec. 3 states that the quality metrics "are intended to serve as a guide for tamper detection difficulty, and not for CMFD performance evaluation per se". Sec. 3 also calls forgery quality estimation "an open question". Sec. 5 concludes that automated CMFD methods perform poorly on COVERAGE. Dataset size is small (100 pairs) and the images are small (Table 1, 400 x 486 average) relative to our 1920x1080 benchmark.

## cozzolino2015

**Full citation:** Davide Cozzolino, Giovanni Poggi, Luisa Verdoliva, "Efficient Dense-Field Copy-Move Forgery Detection", IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security, vol. 10, no. 11, pp. 2284-2297, 2015. DOI 10.1109/TIFS.2015.2455334.

The IEEE Xplore abstract page (https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7154457) was read this session and gives the abstract verbatim plus the opening paragraph of Sec. I only, before the paywall cuts off.
**What the method computes:** From the IEEE abstract (verbatim): "We propose a new algorithm for the accurate detection and localization of copy-move forgeries, based on rotation-invariant features computed densely on the image. Dense-field techniques proposed in the literature guarantee a superior performance with respect to their keypoint-based counterparts, at the price of a much higher processing time, mostly due to the feature matching phase. To overcome this limitation, we resort here to a fast approximate nearest-neighbor search algorithm, PatchMatch, especially suited for the computation of dense fields over images. We adapt the matching algorithm to deal efficiently with invariant features, so as to achieve higher robustness with respect to rotations and scale changes. Moreover, leveraging on the smoothness of the output field, we implement a simplified and reliable postprocessing procedure. The experimental analysis, conducted on databases available online, proves the proposed technique to be at least as accurate, generally more robust, and typically much faster than the state-of-the-art dense-field references."
That is the full extent of what was read directly from this paper. The rest of this entry's algorithmic detail comes from a **different, later paper by the same authors**, D'Amiano, Cozzolino, Poggi, Verdoliva, "A PatchMatch-based Dense-field Algorithm for Video Copy-Move Detection and Localization" (arXiv:1703.04636, local PDF `references/papers/damiano2017_video-copymove.pdf`, read in full this session), whose Sec. II.B is explicitly a recap of "the technique proposed in [27]" where [27] is cited in that paper's own reference list as exactly this cozzolino2015 TIFS paper (damiano2017 ref [27]: "D. Cozzolino, G. Poggi, and L. Verdoliva, 'Efficient dense-field copy-move forgery detection,' IEEE TIFS, vol. 10, no. 11, pp. 2284-2297, November 2015"). Everything below tagged **[via damiano2017 Sec. II.B]** is damiano2017's own description of cozzolino2015's method, not text read from cozzolino2015 itself - treat it as a secondary source, likely accurate (same authors, same lab) but not a substitute for the primary text.
- **[via damiano2017 Sec. II.B]** Feature: image expressed in polar coordinates I(rho,theta); a kernel K_{n,m}(rho,theta) = R_{n,m}(rho) * (1/sqrt(2*pi)) * e^{jm*theta} is formed from a radial profile R_{n,m} and a circular harmonic (damiano2017 Eq. 4); projecting the image onto this kernel gives feature f(n,m) (damiano2017 Eq. 5). Choosing Zernike orthonormal radial functions for R_{n,m} makes f(n,m) the Zernike moment of order (n,m); damiano2017 states "by selecting as features the magnitude of Zernike moments we guarantee rotation invariance", and that using only a few low-order moments gives a compact feature vector "robust to intensity distortions, which are mostly of high-pass nature" (damiano2017 Sec. II.B).
- **[via damiano2017 Sec. II.B]** Matching: standard PatchMatch (Barnes et al. 2009) is modified because the basic algorithm assumes a patchwise-constant offset field (correct for pure translation copy-moves) but rotated/resized copy-moves produce a *linearly varying* offset field. The fix from the earlier Cozzolino/Poggi/Verdoliva ICIP-2014 PatchMatch paper (damiano2017's ref [31]) adds first-order predictors to PatchMatch's usual zero-order neighbor predictors: zero-order prediction ~delta0_x(s) = delta(x) for x in {r,d,c,a} (row/column/diagonal/antidiagonal neighbor) (damiano2017 Eq. 6); first-order prediction ~delta1_x(s) = 2*delta(x) - delta(xx), using a second, farther neighbor xx along the same direction to extrapolate a linear trend (damiano2017 Eq. 7). Both sets together form the enlarged predictor set used in PatchMatch's propagation step (damiano2017 Eq. 8).
- **[via damiano2017 Sec. II.B]** Post-processing / regularisation: called **Dense Linear Fitting (DLF)**. An affine model is fit locally to the offset field around each point, using the data themselves to estimate the affine parameters. damiano2017 states the fit "is typically good in correspondence of a copy-moved region, where the offset field is either constant (for plain copy-moves) or linearly varying (in the presence of rotations or resizing)", while in pristine image areas the offset field is "more chaotic" and the local affine fit is worse. Large areas of low fitting error are then flagged as copy-moves. damiano2017 adds that DLF "is very fast, as it only requires a few linear filtering and products per pixel" - i.e. no combinatorial/RANSAC-style search, the affine fit is a closed-form local linear estimate. damiano2017 Sec. III.D (describing its own, video, algorithm but citing the still-image one as the baseline) separately states that in the still-image method of [27]="cozzolino2015", false alarms surviving DLF "were largely eliminated through morphological filtering" - so the pipeline is dense Zernike features -> modified PatchMatch NNF -> DLF regularisation -> morphological cleanup, with **no keypoints, no RANSAC, and no convex hull anywhere in the chain**.
**How the final mask is built:** Per the reconstruction above **[via damiano2017 Sec. II.B, indirect]**: the mask is the set of pixels where DLF's local affine-fit residual is low, thresholded and then morphologically filtered - a per-pixel dense decision, not a hull or bounding shape over a sparse point set. This is structurally the opposite of our SIFT+convex-hull approach: every pixel gets its own nearest-neighbour offset and its own local-fit quality score, so the output mask can in principle hug an irregular or non-convex duplicated region instead of ballooning to the hull of scattered inlier keypoints. `not verified from retrieved text` for cozzolino2015 itself: the exact threshold(s) used on the DLF fitting-error map, and whatever cozzolino2015's own Sec. numbering calls this step (damiano2017 does not give cozzolino2015's original section/equation numbers, only a prose recap).
**Source vs destination convention:** not verified from retrieved text (neither the IEEE abstract/intro fragment nor damiano2017's recap of the still-image method states whether the output mask marks source, destination, or both; damiano2017's own video method, by contrast, explicitly marks "both original and clones" per its Fig. 6 caption - "In GT (left) copy-moved regions, both original and clones, are set to 1 (red)" - but that is damiano2017's video ground-truth/output convention, not a statement about cozzolino2015's still-image paper, and is flagged here only so it is not mistakenly transferred to cozzolino2015).
**Computational cost:** not verified from retrieved text for cozzolino2015 directly (the IEEE preview cuts off before any complexity discussion). INFERENCE: wen2016 Table 3 lists "Dense-Field (Cozzolino 2015)" as a comparison method and reports it as the most accurate of the three CMFD methods tested (SIFT/SURF/Dense-Field) on 3 of 4 datasets (see wen2016 entry above), which is indirect evidence the method is tractable enough to run as a baseline in another group's benchmark, but wen2016 does not report its runtime either. damiano2017 Sec. III.D gives complexity figures only for its own *video* algorithm (a 3D extension), not for the 2015 still-image method, so those numbers are not transferable here.
**Reported performance:** not verified from retrieved text (the IEEE preview cuts off before the experimental section; the only performance numbers seen this session for "Dense-Field (Cozzolino 2015)" are wen2016's Table 3, already recorded in the wen2016 entry above: COVERAGE 71.8%, CoMoFoD 72.0%, Manipulate 95.8%, GRIP 82.0% pair-discrimination accuracy - note these are wen2016's own re-run numbers on wen2016's benchmark, not numbers cozzolino2015 reported about itself).
**Relevance to replacing our SIFT+convex-hull detector:** High, on the strength of both independent signals available: wen2016 ranks it above SIFT/SURF on 3 of 4 datasets including the closest analogue to our low-texture/occlusive problem, and the method's own abstract explicitly targets exactly the failure mode named in our brief - it claims robustness to rotation and scale changes at lower cost than prior dense-field work, which is the compute concern at 1920x1080. Structurally (per the damiano2017 recap) it replaces both of our detector's weak points at once: dense Zernike-moment matching means no keypoint-starved low-texture region goes undetected, and DLF's per-pixel local-affine-fit thresholding means the mask is built from a per-pixel confidence field rather than the convex hull of a sparse inlier set, directly targeting the imprecision our current detector suffers from. INFERENCE: because the actual cozzolino2015 text was not read, this assessment leans on the damiano2017 recap and wen2016's benchmark numbers rather than primary confirmation; before reimplementing, the DLF fitting-error threshold and the morphological-cleanup parameters (unspecified in every source read this session) would need to be found either in the still-unread body of cozzolino2015 or in the released code at `grip.unina.it/download/prog/CMFD/` (file `CMFD_PM_code.zip`, 6.7 MB, last modified 2023-05-19 per the directory listing fetched this session - not downloaded, as downloading executable/code archives requires explicit user permission and was outside this task's PDF-only scope).
**Limitations the authors themselves state:** not verified from retrieved text (limitations/conclusion section not reachable through the IEEE preview).

## christlein2012

**Full citation:** Vincent Christlein, Christian Riess, Johannes Jordan, Corinna Riess, Elli Angelopoulou, "An Evaluation of Popular Copy-Move Forgery Detection Approaches", IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security, vol. 7, no. 6, pp. 1841-1854, 2012.
**Retrieval:** Downloaded this session from arXiv (arXiv:1208.3665v2, 26 Nov 2012 revision per the arXiv watermark on p.1 of the extracted text) to `references/papers/christlein2012_evaluation.pdf` (26 pp, extraction clean). SHA-256 `585e579efa02402bf4fb296b25677a92a1784ca8a3a0de8717b4ada83a4fd7c5`.
**What the method computes:** This is a *benchmark/evaluation* paper, not a single new detector - it re-implements 15 CMFD feature sets (13 block-based, 2 keypoint-based: SIFT and SURF) inside one shared pipeline and measures each. The shared pipeline (Sec. II, and the six-step summary on p.3): (1) grayscale conversion; (2) feature extraction - block-based methods tile the image into overlapping b x b blocks (b=16, Sec. V-A) and compute one feature vector per block (13 variants: BLUR, HU, ZERNIKE [moment-based]; PCA, SVD, KPCA [dimensionality-reduction]; LUO, BRAVO, LIN, CIRCLE [intensity]; DCT, DWT, FMT [frequency] - grouped in Table I with feature lengths, e.g. ZERNIKE=12, SIFT=128, SURF=64), while keypoint-based methods (SIFT, SURF) extract features only at detected high-entropy keypoints, no image subdivision; (3) matching - approximate nearest neighbour via Muja et al.'s randomized kd-trees (Sec. II, "matching" paragraph), used uniformly instead of the per-paper-original lexicographic sorting or Best-Bin-First, for fair comparison; (4) remove matched pairs whose spatial shift vector has norm below tau_1=50 px (Sec. V-A, "to avoid matches between spatially close, self-correlated regions"); (5) cluster remaining matches that agree on a common affine transform - for block-based methods, count H(A) = number of pairs satisfying the same affine transform A, keep only if H(A) >= tau_2 (feature-specific, calibrated per Fig. 7 and listed in Table II's rightmost column, e.g. ZERNIKE tau_2=800, SIFT/SURF tau_2=4); for keypoint-based methods, the paper's own novel combination (Sec. II "Postprocessing" paragraph, pp.2-3): build Amerini-et-al.-style location clusters but stop merging when nearest-neighbour distance is too high (avoiding Amerini's "reportedly hard to calibrate inconsistency threshold"), estimate an affine transform per cluster pair via RANSAC, refine with "the gold standard algorithm for affine homography matrices"; (6) declare an image tampered if a connected region exceeds tau_3 connected pixels (tau_3 = tau_2 for block methods, tau_3 = 1000 for keypoint methods, Sec. V-A).
**How the final mask is built:** This is the section most directly relevant to us, and it is explicit that the authors did **not** use a convex hull. Quoting Sec. II verbatim (p.3): "To obtain pixel-wise results for keypoint-based methods, we combined the methods of Amerini et al. and Pan and Lyu. We built the clusters described by Amerini et al., but avoided the search for the reportedly hard to calibrate inconsistency threshold [3]. Instead, clusters stop merging when the distance to their nearest neighbors are too high, then the affine transformation between clusters is computed using RANSAC and afterwards refined by applying the gold standard algorithm for affine homography matrices. For each such estimated transform, we computed the correlation map according to Pan and Lyu [22]." So for keypoint-based methods (structurally the closest published analogue to our SIFT pipeline), once RANSAC gives an affine transform, the paper does not paint the hull of the inlier keypoints; instead it warps the whole image (or region) by the estimated transform and computes a dense per-pixel correlation map between the image and its transformed copy (Pan and Lyu 2010's method), thresholds that correlation map, and that thresholded dense map is the pixel-level output compared to ground truth. Block-based methods are dense by construction - every block already carries a feature vector, so their output mask is simply the union of blocks belonging to a surviving H(A)>=tau_2 cluster, again with no hull step. `not verified from retrieved text`: the exact correlation threshold value used with Pan and Lyu's map, and any subsequent morphological cleanup, which the paper does not restate here (referring only to "For full details on our implementation... please refer to the supplemental material", which was not available this session).
**Source vs destination convention:** The ground truth marks a **single combined region**, not separate source/destination masks. Sec. III describes construction: 48 base "source images" each get one or more semantically meaningful "snippets" cut out and pasted elsewhere in the same image; "A ground truth map ... is generated where copy-moved pixels are white, unaltered pixels are black and boundary pixels are gray" (Fig. 3 caption, Sec. III). The text gives no indication that the original (cut-from) location is separately marked in the ground truth - only the pasted/"copied" region in the final tampered image is described as white. INFERENCE: this reads as the same "combined mask" convention that wen2016 attributes to CoMoFoD and GRIP (see wen2016 entry above), i.e. this dataset most likely scores only the destination region, not source-vs-destination separately - flagged INFERENCE because the paper never explicitly states "we do not mark the source region."
**Computational cost:** Table V (p.12, "AVERAGE COMPUTATION TIMES PER IMAGE IN SECONDS") gives per-feature Feature/Matching/Postprocessing/Total times (seconds) and peak memory (MB), averaged over the 3000x2300-px-average dataset (Sec. III: "The average size of an image is about 3000x2300 pixels"). Selected rows: SIFT total 610.96 s (Feature 15.61, Matching 126.15, Postpr. 469.14), mem. 17.18 MB; SURF total 1052.12 s, mem. 19.92 MB; ZERNIKE total 7065.18 s (Feature 2131.27, Matching 4903.59, Postpr. 27.23), mem. 462.03 MB; DCT total 35590.03 s, mem. 9856.67 MB; BLUR total 16712.19 s, mem. 924.06 MB; dataset average across all 15 methods: Feature 3549.41 s, Matching 4829.22 s, Postpr. 105.72 s, Total 8487.63 s, Mem. 2266.43 MB (Table V last row). Sec. VI states plainly: "SURF and SIFT, as keypoint-based methods, excel in computation time, memory footprint. The advantage in speed is so significant, that we consider it worth applying these methods always, independent of the detection goal" and "Keypoint-based methods excel in computation time and memory consumption... the number of extracted keypoints is typically an order of magnitude smaller than the number of image blocks" (Sec. V-F, p.11-12). At our target resolution (1920x1080, smaller than this paper's ~3000x2300 average), these SIFT numbers are the closest available upper bound for what our own detector's matching cost looks like, and the >4,000 s block-based Zernike matching time (Table V) is the cost of the alternative the paper recommends.
**Reported performance:** Table II (p.7, "RESULTS FOR PLAIN COPY-MOVE AT IMAGE LEVEL IN PERCENT", no postprocessing, 48 tampered + 48 original images, per-method optimal tau_2): ZERNIKE Precision 92.31 / Recall 100.00 / F1 96.00 (tau_2=800); SIFT Precision 88.37 / Recall 79.17 / F1 83.52 (tau_2=4); SURF Precision 91.49 / Recall 89.58 / F1 90.53 (tau_2=4); best F1 overall in this table is LIN at 96.97. Average across all 15 methods: Precision 85.54, Recall 97.92, F1 90.98 (Table II last row). Robustness experiments (Sec. V-B-5, pp.7-9, not tabulated with exact numbers extracted here beyond prose): under Gaussian noise up to sigma=0.10, "7 out of 15 features drop to under 50% recall rate" while DCT, PCA, SIFT, SURF and HU "maintain their good recall"; under rotation, "ZERNIKE performed remarkably well" (Sec. VI) and at pixel level "SIFT and SURF" lose ground for "larger amounts of rotation" (Sec. V-D-5, line ~1292-1295) while "for the special case of" - text truncated in extraction at that point. Sec. VI's overall recommendation: use a **two-component system** - a keypoint-based method (SIFT/SURF) for fast large-scale screening, plus a block-based method (ZERNIKE recommended specifically, "due to its relatively small memory footprint") for close, reliable examination of a flagged image (Sec. VI, p.13, "In a practical setup, one should consider a two-component CMFD system...We consider ZERNIKE features as a good choice for this component").
**Relevance to replacing our SIFT+convex-hull detector:** Very high, for two distinct reasons. First, this paper is direct, quantitative evidence that our exact keypoint family (SIFT) is the fastest option but has the worst F1 (83.52) of the three methods it reports in Table II, confirming our own observed weakness is not implementation-specific but a structural property of keypoint-based CMFD on low-texture/low-keypoint-density content (Sec. VI: "keypoint-based methods... are sensitive to low-contrast regions and repetitive image content"). Second, and most actionable: this paper's own solution to "how do you get a pixel mask out of RANSAC-fit keypoint clusters" is *not* a convex hull - it is Pan and Lyu's dense correlation map computed after applying the fitted affine transform to the whole image, which is a very different, far more precise family of technique that could replace only our mask-construction step while keeping SIFT keypoint matching (i.e. a minimal-diff fix: keep SIFT+RANSAC for finding the transform, replace convex-hull-of-inliers with a transform-then-correlate dense pass). The paper's overall recommendation of a two-component system (fast SIFT/SURF screening + slower ZERNIKE block-based confirmation) is also a directly actionable architecture suggestion for a system, like ours, that must run at 1920x1080.
**Limitations the authors themselves state:** Sec. VI, "Discussion": block-based methods (including the recommended ZERNIKE) do not scale well - "running a block-based CMFD algorithm on full-sized images can easily exceed the available resources" and downsampling to save time causes "overall performance drops" though "the best performing feature sets remain relatively stable" at a lower level. Keypoint-based methods "lack the detail for highly accurate detection results" and "are prone to miss" low-structure copied regions, while "highly self-similar image content...can provoke false positive matches" (Sec. VI). Ground truth itself is stated to be imperfect at boundaries: "accurate ground truth is difficult to define in such mixed data regions" (semi-transparent/smeared snippet edges, Sec. III) and such boundary pixels are excluded from pixel-level scoring rather than resolved (Sec. IV-B). The paper also notes its own dataset choices are debatable: image size and interpolation both measurably affect results (Sec. VI, referencing Fig. 12), so any cross-paper performance comparison (including against wen2016's numbers recorded above) is sensitive to exactly which image sizes were tested.

## ryu2013

**Full citation:** Seung-Jin Ryu, Matthias Kirchner, Min-Jeong Lee, Heung-Kyu Lee, "Rotation Invariant Localization of Duplicated Image Regions Based on Zernike Moments", IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security, vol. 8, no. 8, pp. 1355-1370, August 2013. DOI 10.1109/TIFS.2013.2272377.
**Retrieval:** Downloaded this session via Wayback Machine snapshot (`web.archive.org/web/20220618024701/https://www1.icsi.berkeley.edu/pubs/vision/ICSI_rotationinvariantlocalization13.pdf` - the live ICSI Berkeley URL linked from Matthias Kirchner's own publications page, `ws.binghamton.edu/kirchner/`, returned HTTP 403 directly, so the archived copy of that same author-hosted file was used) to `references/papers/ryu2013_zernike.pdf` (16 pp, extraction clean). SHA-256 `37e216aeee3daa61fa7b419222e6dc580b5edcf56f699b22b7658aab4da9d3a2`.
**What the method computes:** A block-based, rotation-invariant copy-**rotate**-move (CRM) detector built on three named contributions (Sec. I-D): (1) **Feature**: overlapping b x b blocks (b=32, Sec. V-A) are each represented by the magnitude of their Zernike moments up to a chosen order n_max (giving feature vectors of stated length per Sec. V-A parameters), exploiting the analytical property, derived in Sec. III-B (Eq. 7-12), that rotating an image by angle theta only phase-shifts each Zernike moment A_{n,m} -> A_{n,m}*e^{-jm*theta}, so the *magnitude* |A_{n,m}| is rotation-invariant by construction - this is the direct mathematical justification damiano2017's recap of cozzolino2015 alludes to more informally. (2) **Matching**: instead of exhaustive or kd-tree search, blocks are matched via locality-sensitive hashing (LSH, Sec. IV-B) using quantized random projections h(v) = floor((a.v + b)/w) with a drawn i.i.d. Gaussian, giving a matching whose complexity (Sec. IV-D Eq. 37) depends on the number of hash collisions rather than exhaustively on block count; a minimum spatial distance threshold between matched blocks is also enforced (Eq. 29) to suppress self-matches. (3) **Error reduction / geometric verification (Algorithm 1, Sec. IV-C)**: unlike prior work that runs RANSAC directly on block/keypoint image-plane coordinates, this paper first estimates a candidate rotation angle theta from each matched pair's own Zernike *phase* difference (Eq. 30), buckets pairs by candidate angle, keeps a pair only if a spatially adjacent block pair supports the same angle, and only then runs RANSAC (on this pre-filtered, phase-consistent set) to fit a 2x2 affine matrix per triple of colinear block-pair correspondences, using polar decomposition (SVD) to separate the fitted matrix into a stretch and a rotation component and checking both that the stretch is near-identity and the rotation matches the candidate angle (Eq. 32-36). "The algorithm outputs the set of pairings with the largest number of inliers as duplicated region" (Sec. IV-C, immediately after Algorithm 1's pseudocode).
**How the final mask is built:** Directly from the surviving inlier block-pair set - **no convex hull, and no separate polygon-fitting step of any kind**. Each RANSAC inlier is a *pair* of matched blocks (b_i, b_j) at two different image locations; the output binary mask is the union of block footprints belonging to inlier pairs at both ends of each pair (Sec. IV-C: "The algorithm outputs the set of pairings with the largest number of inliers as duplicated region"; Fig. 3 caption similarly labels the output "(c) detected region" opposite the ground truth "(b) duplicated region"). Because the primitive matched unit is a fixed b x b block rather than a sparse keypoint, and every block in the image already carries a feature vector (dense-on-a-grid, though at block rather than pixel granularity), the detected region naturally hugs the true tampered footprint far more tightly than a hull over a handful of surviving SIFT inliers would - this is the same structural advantage the dense-field cozzolino2015 line of work has over keypoint methods, achieved here at the coarser cost of one Zernike vector per block rather than one per pixel. `not verified from retrieved text`: any morphological post-cleanup after the RANSAC output (the paper's Sec. IV-D explicitly declines to analyze cost "consequently relatively low compared to feature extraction and matching, and we do not consider the time consumed by this last step any further" - suggesting the paper treats Algorithm 1's raw inlier-block union as the final map, with no extra step described).
**Source vs destination convention:** Because the detection primitive is a matched *pair* of blocks (source block and destination/copy block), and Algorithm 1's stated output is "the set of pairings ... as duplicated region" (plural pairings, not a single-sided list), INFERENCE: the detected/output mask marks **both** ends of every inlier pair - i.e., both the original and the copied location - by construction, not just the pasted region. This is stated with an INFERENCE tag because the paper never explicitly writes "we mark both source and destination"; it is a structural reading of "pairings...as duplicated region" combined with Fig. 3's single "duplicated region" ground-truth panel (b), which itself is ambiguous about whether it covers one or both locations. For the "real-life manipulations" experiment (Sec. V-H), ground truth comes from Christlein et al.'s Erlangen Image Manipulation Dataset - the same dataset described in the christlein2012 entry above, whose ground truth was read this session as a **single combined mask** ("copy-moved pixels are white", no separate source/destination masks) - so on that particular sub-experiment, the evaluation convention is inherited from christlein2012's dataset, not independently defined by this paper.
**Computational cost:** Sec. IV-D gives an analytical complexity for feature extraction and matching (Eq. 37): time up to the matching step is on the order of computing Zernike polynomials (roughly O(block-area) per moment) plus LSH hashing/search cost, which "depends, for a fixed image size and number of features, on the number of hash collisions, and thus on the image content" - no closed-form total is given. Sec. V-G / Table VII reports "average run times of the tested detectors" for "each 1000 images of size 512x512" (page 13/1368): the extracted PDF text preserves the prose around Table VII but not its numeric cell values (a table-extraction gap, not a redaction) - prose states "Among the tested block-based methods, our detector is fastest, outperforming others by a factor of 2 at least" and "all these detectors are considerably slower than SIFT-based methods" (Sec. V-G). Exact seconds: `not verified from retrieved text` (table values did not survive text extraction). Test images throughout the paper's own experiments are only 512x512 (Sec. V-A/C), far below our 1920x1080 target, so no cost figure here is a direct estimate at our resolution; INFERENCE: since Zernike-per-block cost scales with the number of b x b blocks (~ image area / b^2) and LSH matching cost scales with hash-collision count (data-dependent, Eq. 37), cost should scale at least linearly and likely worse-than-linearly with image area, meaning the ~4x wider, ~2x taller area at 1920x1080 (roughly 8x the pixels of 512x512) would multiply this paper's own 512x512 runtimes by a similarly large but unquantified factor.
**Reported performance:** Sec. V-C, Table I/II (median PDA/PFP by rotation angle, textured/smooth subsets, 512x512 images, 96x96 duplicated regions, 500 manipulations per class): "algorithms based on Zernike moments appear to be the most accurate approaches" across all tested rotation angles (0-90 deg in 10 deg steps), and the proposed LSH+phase-based method "outperforms prior art especially for smooth duplicated regions" with "generally lower PFP values than SATS" (Christlein et al.'s Zernike/SATS combination, i.e. this paper still beats a Zernike-based competitor, not just non-Zernike ones) (Sec. V-C). Pan and Lyu's SIFT-based method "operates very well on the textured set, yet...relatively lower PDA values for smooth duplicated regions, where it is hard to extract a sufficient number of key points" (Sec. V-C) - direct, independent confirmation of exactly the low-texture failure mode our brief names for SIFT. On the Erlangen real-manipulation test set (Sec. V-H, Table VI), "Zernike moments offer one of the best trade-offs between high PDA and relatively low PFP" and beat SATS, Wang et al., Bashar et al. (DWT), and Pan and Lyu on PFP at similar PDA, except on one image ('building') with a duplicated region smaller than the 32x32 block size, which no detector localized well. Exact PDA/PFP percentages in Tables I, II, IV, VI: `not verified from retrieved text` (numeric table cells did not survive PDF text extraction, only the surrounding prose and captions did).
**Relevance to replacing our SIFT+convex-hull detector:** High. This is a second, independent confirmation (alongside christlein2012 in this file) that SIFT/keypoint-based CMFD specifically fails on smooth/low-texture duplicated regions because "it is hard to extract a sufficient number of key points" (Sec. V-C) - exactly our stated failure mode. Structurally, this paper's mask-construction approach (union of inlier block footprints from phase-verified, RANSAC-confirmed block pairs) is a second working example, independent from cozzolino2015/damiano2017, of building a tight mask without any hull step, achieved here by keeping the matching unit small and grid-aligned (blocks) rather than sparse (keypoints) - reinforcing that the fix for our convex-hull problem is architectural (match on a dense-ish grid, mark matched-pair footprints directly) rather than a matter of tuning RANSAC. The phase-based error-reduction trick (Sec. IV-C) - estimating rotation from each matched pair's own Zernike phase before running RANSAC, rather than trusting RANSAC alone to find rotation - is a specific, reusable idea for tightening false-positive control if we ever add a rotation-invariant block or dense feature to our own pipeline.
**Limitations the authors themselves state:** Sec. VI, "Concluding Remarks", verbatim: "detectors based on Zernike moments are inherently incapable of localizing duplicated regions that underwent strong affine transformations other than rotation" (i.e. no scale invariance by design - confirmed separately in Sec. V-F/Table V, where "very strong scaling impairs the performance of all detectors, most apparently when smooth duplicated regions are concerned"). The paper also states a broader open question: "it remains an open research question whether there exists one single 'catch-all' approach, or whether combining specialized detectors...is more favorable" (Sec. VI), and flags automated decision-making from the binary output map as a weak point distinct from localization itself: "rather simple computational measures, such as plain integration over the output map, ignore available semantical information. They are thus particularly prone to false alarms" (Sec. VI) - i.e. even a good per-pixel mask does not by itself solve image-level tamper/no-tamper decisions reliably.

## amerini2013

**Full citation:** Irene Amerini, Lamberto Ballan, Roberto Caldelli, Alberto Del Bimbo, Luca Del Tongo, Giuseppe Serra, "Copy-move forgery detection and localization by means of robust clustering with J-Linkage", Signal Processing: Image Communication, vol. 28, no. 6, pp. 659-669, 2013. DOI 10.1016/j.image.2013.03.006.
**Retrieval:** Downloaded this session from the MICC (Media Integration and Communication Center, University of Florence) authors' own site, `http://www.micc.unifi.it/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/2013_copy_move.pdf` (found via that lab's public project page, `micc.unifi.it/projects/mm-forensics/`), to `references/papers/amerini2013_jlinkage.pdf` (11 pp, extraction clean). SHA-256 `399407e12c2f7bcdc4a7c50ffa8c6ce8aca945b989f6dc354d09f8015822636f`.
**What the method computes:** SIFT keypoints and descriptors are extracted and matched using g2NN, this paper's (and its predecessor Amerini et al. 2011's) generalization of Lowe's 2NN ratio test to allow multiple matches per keypoint (Sec. 3.1): the sorted distance ratios d_i/d_{i+1} are examined in sequence and matches are accepted up to the first ratio exceeding threshold tau=0.5 (as opposed to a single ratio-test cutoff), which is explicitly designed to handle a region copied more than once. The core contribution is the second stage (Sec. 3.2): rather than clustering matched-keypoint *coordinates* directly (which the paper explicitly names as our failure mode, see below), an adaptation of the **J-Linkage algorithm** clusters matched pairs *in the space of the geometric transformation* they support. m=500 random affine-transformation hypotheses are generated from randomly sampled minimal point sets (each hypothesis fit by least-squares over k+1=3 correspondences, Eq. 4); each matched pair p is assigned a binary preference-set vector PS(p) in {0,1}^m marking which hypotheses it is an inlier of (Eq. 1); pairs are then hierarchically agglomeratively clustered using Jaccard distance between preference sets (Eq. 2), merging until preference sets no longer overlap (cut-off = 1); each surviving cluster's final transformation is refined by least-squares over all its member pairs; clusters supported by fewer than a fixed threshold N of matched pairs are discarded as outliers (N tuned via ROC in Sec. 4.3, best value N=9). An image is declared forged if at least one such transformation survives (Sec. 3.2, confirmed Sec. 4.2: "an image is labeled as forged if at least an affine transformation is estimated between a couple of image regions"). The paper's own novel minimal-sample-set selection strategy (Sec. 3.2.1) additionally biases J-Linkage's random sampling toward spatially coherent neighborhoods (window w=12 nearest neighbours around each of the two points of a seed pair) rather than Kanazawa et al.'s original distance-decay sampling, and is shown (Table 1) to improve TPR from 76.61% to 81.60% at N=9 on MICC-F600 versus the original J-Linkage sampling.
**How the final mask is built:** This is the second independent example in this file (after christlein2012's use of Pan and Lyu's method) of a published CMFD paper that explicitly rejects hull/spatial clustering in favour of a dense transform-and-correlate step, and it is unusually explicit about *why*. Sec. 3.2 states the motivation directly: clustering "on the coordinates of the keypoints such as a hierarchical agglomerative clustering (HAC) procedure as in [Amerini et al. 2011]...has two main drawbacks: (i) the inability to separate duplicated regions that are close to each other and (ii) the difficulty to identify a patch as single, when it contains keypoints with a non-uniform spatial distribution" - this is essentially our exact convex-hull failure mode, named and cited as the reason to abandon spatial clustering. Given the transformation H estimated for a surviving cluster (Sec. 3.3, "Localizing duplicated regions"), the method applies H to the *entire image* to get a warped image W (Eq. 5: R_D = H*R_O, and the inverse R_O = H^{-1}*R_D - both directions computed), then computes a dense **zero-mean normalized cross-correlation (ZNCC)** map between the original grayscale image I and the warped image W over a 7-pixel neighbourhood Omega(x) around every pixel x (Eq. 6). This correlation map is Gaussian-filtered (size 7, sigma=0.5) to reduce noise, thresholded at th=0.55 into a binary map, small isolated regions under 0.05% of image area are discarded, and morphological operations fill remaining holes (Sec. 3.3, describing Fig. 2c-d). So the pipeline is: SIFT+g2NN match -> J-Linkage cluster in transform space -> per-cluster affine H -> whole-image warp -> dense ZNCC correlation map -> Gaussian smooth -> threshold -> small-region removal -> morphological fill. At no point is a hull or bounding polygon of inlier keypoints used; the mask comes entirely from a dense per-pixel correlation test after the transform is known.
**Source vs destination convention:** Explicitly **both**. Eq. 5 computes both R_D = H*R_O (forward) and R_O = H^{-1}*R_D (inverse), and Sec. 4.3.2's qualitative discussion states directly: "the technique is able to accurately segment source and destination patches, also in very challenging cases" (Fig. 8 examples, including cases where "the original and the cloned area are very close to each other"). The FP/FN localization metric formalizes this: R_1 is defined as "the copied region" and R_i (i>1) "the i-th duplicated region" summed together as the positive set (Eq. 7-8), i.e. ground truth and detection both cover the full set of tampered regions (source and destination, and all copies in multi-cloning cases) as one union, not scored as two separate source/destination masks - similar in spirit to the "combined mask" convention flagged in the wen2016 and christlein2012 entries above, except here the detector itself is confirmed (not just inferred) to actually mark both locations.
**Computational cost:** Sec. 4, explicit and directly quotable: "our approach is able to effectively detect and localize copy-move forgery in a full-resolution image (e.g. 800x600 pixels) in around 8 s on an Intel Q6600 with 4-GB RAM," contrasted with two block-based competitors on the same images at "around 295 s and 71 s, respectively, according to our previous experiments on processing time requirements" (Sec. 4, citing the authors' 2011 paper). This is the most concrete, apples-to-apples runtime figure found across all papers processed this session, though the test resolution (800x600) is well below our 1920x1080 target and the hardware (Intel Q6600, a 2007-era quad-core, 4 GB RAM) is dated; INFERENCE: since this is fundamentally still a sparse-keypoint pipeline (SIFT extraction/matching cost, which dominates block-based alternatives' cost profile, scales with keypoint count rather than pixel count) plus one dense ZNCC pass over the full image for localization (which does scale with pixel count, ~4x more pixels at 1920x1080 than 800x600, using a small fixed 7x7 window so still cheap per-pixel), the ZNCC localization step is the part of this pipeline most directly comparable in cost to what a convex-hull-replacement pass would cost if grafted onto our own SIFT detector - `not verified from retrieved text` for a decomposed feature-extraction-vs-ZNCC time split, so this inference cannot be checked against the paper's own numbers.
**Reported performance:** MICC-F600 (600 images, 448 original / 152 forged, resolutions 800x533 to 3888x2592, Sec. 4.1), best operating point N=9 (Sec. 4.3): TPR 81.60%, FPR 7.27%, patch localization FP 0.31%, FN 6.59% (also see the N=8..10 sweep in Table 1, comparing the paper's own sampling strategy against Kanazawa et al.'s: at N=9, TPR 81.60 vs 76.61, FPR 7.27 vs 8.68, FP 0.31 vs 0.24, FN 6.59 vs 8.47). Versus the authors' own earlier spatial-clustering method (Amerini et al. 2011, "HAC"), Table 2: on MICC-F600, TPR improves 69.0% -> 81.6% and FPR improves 12.5% -> 7.27%; on the easier MICC-F2000 dataset, TPR 93.42% -> 94.86%, FPR 11.61% -> 9.15% - i.e. replacing spatial clustering with transform-domain clustering is worth roughly 12 points of TPR and 5 points of FPR on the harder dataset, with a smaller but still positive gain on the easier one (Sec. 4.4). On the SATS-130 rotation dataset (Table 3, patch localization only, no authenticity/original images available for that dataset per Sec. 4.1), the proposed method achieves "FP around zero and a FN of 16.34%", versus three benchmark block-based methods reported at FP/FN of 49%/6% (INT2), 24%/66% (INT4), and 0.4%/88% (MOM3) - i.e. every block-based competitor tested there trades badly between FP and FN, while this method gets both low simultaneously (Sec. 4.5, Table 3).
**Relevance to replacing our SIFT+convex-hull detector:** Very high, and the most directly actionable paper found this session for a minimal-diff fix. This is the closest published method to our own pipeline (SIFT extraction, ratio-test-style matching, affine geometric verification of matched pairs) that explicitly diagnoses and fixes the exact failure named in our brief - spatial clustering (structurally similar to convex-hulling an inlier point set) failing when "the copied patch contains pixels that are spatially very distant among them" or "when the pasted area is near to the original source" (Abstract, Sec. 1). Because the fix (J-Linkage clustering in transform space, then whole-image-warp-plus-ZNCC for the mask) slots in *after* SIFT keypoint matching and *before* mask output, it is a drop-in replacement for exactly the two stages our detector currently gets wrong (RANSAC/clustering, and hull-based mask construction) without discarding our existing SIFT front end. It is also the only paper in this file with a directly comparable, favourable full-pipeline runtime figure (~8 s at 800x600 on 2007-era hardware) suggesting the added ZNCC dense pass is not itself the computational bottleneck.
**Limitations the authors themselves state:** None stated as an explicit "Limitations" subsection - Sec. 5 ("Conclusion") is affirmative rather than self-critical, stating only that "results confirm that the proposed method outperforms other similar state-of-the-art techniques". The one self-acknowledged failure case is qualitative, in Sec. 4.4/Fig. 9: when "the source and destination areas [are] too close to be distinguished as separated," the image can still be "wrongly assigned as authentic" even with the improved transform-domain clustering, i.e. very-close-together copy-moves remain a failure mode, just a rarer one than with the authors' own earlier spatial-clustering method. `not verified from retrieved text` for any other explicitly authors-stated limitation (e.g. no discussion found of behaviour under heavy JPEG compression, noise, or very small duplicated regions, which are common failure axes raised by other papers in this file).
